# Solution brief

A solution brief is the internal agreement on what a feature is, who it is for, and how to talk about it. It feeds one-pagers, decks, talk tracks, and help articles downstream. A fact that is wrong here propagates everywhere, so the brief never invents behavior, pricing, or competitor claims. Unknowns get flagged, not filled.

## When to answer from a brief instead of building one

People ask positioning questions ("what is our angle on X") more often than they ask for a brief. Answer in three steps:

1. Search for an existing brief on that feature.
2. If one exists, quote it, name it, and note its date. Offer a refresh if it is stale.
3. If none exists, answer what you can and say no brief exists, then offer to build one.

Never answer a positioning question from general knowledge when a brief exists. The brief is the agreed position.

## Section order

1. **Product name**
2. **Solution overview.** Two paragraphs: what it is in plain language, then who it is for and what breaks without it. No feature lists here.
3. **Target audience.** The personas this feature touches, one line each on what they own or need.
4. **Marketing messages.** Three or four taglines, then a short campaign story. Outcome language, no feature names in taglines.
5. **Sales talk tracks.** One row per persona: pain, goal, value.
6. **Use cases.** Five to seven rows, each a concrete example a rep could say out loud. Include one edge case that differentiates.
7. **Customer journey and FAQs.** Standing questions first, then feature-specific ones.
8. **Competitive positioning.** Name the clearest point-solution competitor, a "why we win" line, then a dated comparison table.
9. **Materials.** A two-column table of everything that already exists, with links. Missing items stay listed as "coming soon" so the gap is visible.

## The value-prop rule

The value-prop column says what changes for that persona's job or business once the pain is gone. It never says what the feature does; the mechanism belongs in use cases and FAQs.

Run every value prop through this test:

- If the sentence is mostly verbs the product performs (flags, surfaces, drafts, reads, answers, shows), it is a feature. Rewrite it.
- If it could be pasted unchanged into the use-case table, it is a feature. Rewrite it.
- It should make sense to a boss who does not care how the feature works.
- It ties back to the pain in the same row.

A feature sentence names capabilities. A value sentence names the outcome for that persona.

## Building workflow

1. **Scope and check** for an existing brief. One brief per feature; refresh in place rather than creating "v2".
2. **Gather from sources of truth** in parallel: the template, personas, product truth, competitive view, and existing materials.
3. **Write** in the fixed section order. Date the competitive section. Mark anything unverifiable with a confirm-with flag.
4. **File** it where the org keeps briefs and register it on the index.
5. **Report back** in chat: link, a five-line summary, and the count of open flags. Do not paste the whole brief; the document is the deliverable.

## Guardrails

- Never invent behavior, pricing, or competitor claims. "Not published" beats an estimate.
- Persona names come from the org's persona list and are not editable here.
- Draft until reviewed. This skill never marks a brief final on its own.
